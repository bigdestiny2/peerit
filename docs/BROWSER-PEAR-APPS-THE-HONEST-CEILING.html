Browser Pear Apps:
The Honest Ceiling

Can you really run a decentralized Pear app in a normal browser, blind over HiveRelay? Yes — and taken as far as the architecture allows, it gets shockingly close to full-P2P quality. There is one hard ceiling, and it's a platform limit, not a code problem.

Live state (this week)

The blind substrate is being deployed on 2 relays right now, with peerit standing up on it with seed content. This doc describes the full target architecture — what becomes possible as each piece lands. Every claim is badged with its current status. Don't promote a claim past its badge.

One thing to internalize before reading

"P2P app in a browser" is doing too much work in most conversations. There are actually four independent axes bundled into that phrase — trust, data, storage, and networking — and a browser can hit three of them fully. The fourth (no relay in the path) is impossible for any browser, on any stack, forever. This doc separates them so you can reason about what's real instead of arguing past the ceiling.

01The short answer

Yes. You can build full Pear apps that run in any browser and are blind over HiveRelay. The browser is always a client of the relay network, never a peer in the DHT — but "blind client of a blind relay network" achieves most of what makes decentralization matter.

Here's the version that's honest and still exciting:

A browser-based Pear app on HiveRelay can be built so that no relay can forge content, read what it holds, attribute it to an app or author, or be seized as a single point of failure. The browser holds the keys, verifies every signature, and assembles the feed locally. The relay's role shrinks to carrying opaque encrypted bytes between parties who chose to be online.

The one thing it cannot do: eliminate the relay entirely. Two browsers behind NAT cannot introduce each other without a third party. That's not a HiveRelay limitation — it's a hard limit of the web platform, and it applies to every "decentralized web" project equally, whether they admit it or not.

The reframe that matters

The honest question isn't "can the browser be a peer?" (no) — it's "can the relay be made blind enough that it doesn't matter?" (mostly yes). This doc is about how far "mostly yes" goes.

02The four axes of "decentralized"

Separate these, and the whole conversation gets clearer.

When people say "decentralized app," they usually mean all four of these at once. But they're independent. A browser-based Pear app hits three fully and one not at all:

Why the distinction matters

Most "decentralized web" projects conflate these axes. They claim "P2P" because they hit axis 1 (signatures) and axis 2 (distributed data) but quietly depend on a central server for axis 4 (networking) and don't even attempt axis 3 (storage blindness). The Pear + HiveRelay stack is unusual in that it does attempt axis 3 seriously — and is honest about axis 4 being out of reach for browsers.

The thing that actually defines "decentralized" in the load-bearing sense — can the server forge content, can it be seized, can it be compelled to edit your words — is axes 1 and 3. Both are achievable in a browser. Axis 4 is the one piece that's genuinely different, and even it can be made blind if not absent.

Seven of nine capabilities work in a browser today or are in active cutover. One more is coded but gated on operators. The last two — the DHT ones — are the ceiling, and only one of those (the tunneled version) is reachable at all.

04The five rungs to the ceiling

Each rung is achievable. Each shrinks what the relay knows. Stack them and you arrive at the strongest defensible browser-based architecture.

1
Blind storage — the substrate Relay holds opaque cells at random addresses. Can't read content, can't enumerate the graph, can't tell peerit apart from any other app. Browser reads/writes over HTTPS. Relay sees your IP and sees that some opaque cell was requested.
Cutover
2
BlindShard body dispersal Post bodies are PVSS-split across a cohort of independent operators. No single relay holds a readable or complete body. The browser does all gathering and reassembly locally. Reaching this rung needs operators, not code.
Coded · needs ≥3 ops
3
Split transport — OHTTP Separate the ingress (sees your IP, can't see the request) from the gateway (sees the request, can't see your IP). Hides the IP/storage correlation. Standard RFC 9458/9180 — not invented here.
Spec'd
4
dht-relay-ws bridge Browser reaches the actual Hyperswarm DHT through a WSS bridge carrying opaque Noise-encrypted frames. The browser still isn't a DHT node — but the bridge is reduced to a dumb byte-pipe that stores nothing readable and serves nothing semantic. Closest a browser gets to "real P2P."
Coded · gated upstream
5
Pure pipe end state Founder's box stops being an /api aggregator. Multiple independent dht-relay-ws endpoints exist; readers pick any. Browser's view assembled locally from many blind sources. No single server holds "the feed."
Spec'd · end state

The compounding effect

Each rung doesn't just add a feature — it shrinks what the relay knows. Stacking rungs 1–3 means: the relay can't read content (1), can't hold complete bodies (2), and can't correlate your IP to your reads (3). By rung 4, the relay is a dumb pipe that moves encrypted frames. By rung 5, there's no single relay at all — just a fleet of blind byte-pipes, any of which can be replaced.

That's a categorically different trust model than "the server owns the data." It just isn't the same as "no relay exists," which is what PearBrowser gives you.

05The one wall that doesn't move

No matter how far you push rungs 1–5, a normal browser will always need at least one reachable relay to make the first hop.

This isn't a HiveRelay limitation. It's not a peerit limitation. It's a hard architectural limit of the web platform itself:

  • HyperDHT is UDP. Browsers cannot open UDP sockets — full stop. WebRTC has UDP, but HyperDHT uses its own protocol, not WebRTC's.
  • DHT membership requires listening. To be a real DHT node you have to accept inbound routing traffic. Browsers can't listen on any port.
  • The first-hop problem. Two browsers behind NAT cannot find each other without a third party to introduce them. There's no pure-P2P bootstrap from a cold browser.

The honest target, stated precisely

A normal browser is never a standalone DHT node. There is always a relay in the path. The honest target is a blind relay, not no relay.

If you remember one sentence from this document, remember that. Every "browser runs a real DHT node" or "no relay in the path" claim — for normal browsers — is false and must not be made. The relay's role can be shrunk until it carries only opaque bytes, but it cannot be removed.

What PearBrowser gets that browsers don't

PearBrowser escapes this limit because it runs on the Pear/Bare runtime — a native process with real UDP sockets, real Hyperswarm participation, real DHT membership. In PearBrowser:

  • You are a peer. You hold routing slots. You serve blocks to other peers.
  • There is no relay in your path. You reach other peers directly over Hyperswarm.
  • The site is content-addressed (hyper://), zero origin, nothing to seize.

This is why PearBrowser is "the strongest trust tier" in every diagram. It's the only mode where axis 4 (network topology) is fully achieved. Normal browsers get axes 1–3 and a blind version of the relay's role in axis 4.

06Claims you can and can't make

The contract. If you can't defend it in front of a hostile auditor, don't say it.

What you CAN say (present tense, defensible today or as cutover lands)

  1. "Your keys never leave your browser. Every record is signed and re-verified locally." Shipped
  2. "The relay holds opaque ciphertext at random addresses. It cannot read your content, enumerate your social graph, or tell our app apart from any other." As substrate lands
  3. "Post bodies are split across independent operators. No single relay holds a readable or complete copy." When ≥3 operators join
  4. "Your IP can be hidden from the storage relay via split transport." When OHTTP ships
  5. "There is no central database to subpoena — only blind fragments across independent operators." At full deployment
  6. "The browser assembles your feed locally from signed records. No server decides what you see." Shipped
  7. "The app bundle is verifiable against an offline signing key — mirrors and auditors can confirm authenticity without trusting the origin." Shipped

What you CANNOT say — ever

Forbidden claims

  • "The browser is a peer in the network." It isn't. It's a blind client of a blind relay network. PearBrowser is a peer; Chrome is not.
  • "There's no relay in the path." There always is, for browsers. The relay can be made blind, not absent.
  • "The operator can't read your posts." For public content, an operator who joins as a reader can reconstruct. Blindness is deniability, not secrecy.
  • "It's anonymous." Metadata leaks: sizes, timing, IPs (without Tor), access patterns.
  • "It works if every relay goes down." It doesn't. Browsers need at least one reachable relay to make the first hop.
  • "It's serverless." It's not, for browsers. It's blind-relay-served, which is different. Reserve "serverless" for PearBrowser.
  • "Browser-to-browser with no intermediary." Impossible behind NAT. Two browsers cannot introduce themselves.

The strongest honest one-liner

"A browser-based Pear app on HiveRelay can be built so that no relay can forge your content, read what it holds, attribute it to an app or author, or be seized as a single point of failure. Your keys, your signatures, your locally-assembled feed. The relay's role is reduced to carrying opaque encrypted bytes between parties who chose to be online."

That is the strongest defensible claim. Everything past it is either a future-tense conditional ("when X ships, Y") or a fantasy.
