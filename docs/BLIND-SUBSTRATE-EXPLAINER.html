The Blind Substrate,
explained for humans

A storage layer that holds your app's data without being able to read it. No marketing, no magic — just a strict rule about what the relay is allowed to know, and a set of locked boxes that make the rule real.

Protocol: hiverelay-blind/1 For: curious users, not engineers Honesty: load-bearing

Terminology: why we say "relay," not "server"

You'll see the word relay throughout this document, not server. That's deliberate and load-bearing. The three words mean very different things:

  • Server — owns and interprets your data; you trust it for truth. peerit never has one of these, in any mode.
  • Relay — carries blind bytes it can't read; a dumb pipe with receipts, no authority. This is what HiveRelay is. A browser user always has one in their path; a PearBrowser user does not.
  • Serverless — no process in the path at all; pure peer-to-peer. True only for PearBrowser. Reserve this word for the native path, never for browsers.

The distinction matters because calling a relay a "server" implies trust and authority that the architecture explicitly rejects — and calling the browser path "serverless" implies no relay is needed, which is false. The honest framing: peerit is always serverless in the trust sense; it is serverless in the topology sense only in PearBrowser.

01The problem this fixes

Most "decentralized" apps still have a dirty secret: the relay can read your data.

Even apps that encrypt the content of your posts usually leave the shape of the data in plaintext. The relay can't read what you wrote, but it can read who you are, which community you posted in, who you replied to, and when. From those keys alone — without ever decrypting a single value — an operator can reconstruct the entire social graph:

This isn't a hypothetical. The first version of peerit's relay did exactly this, and the creator almost didn't notice — until an audit pointed out the relay was effectively a complete social-graph oracle. Who talks to whom, in which community, how often, all readable from key prefixes alone.

The core realization

Blindness is a constraint on the storage shape, not a checkbox on the payload. Encrypting the post body is the easy part. The hard part — and the part that actually matters — is making sure the storage keys, the request paths, and the metadata don't leak the graph.

02The big idea: a strict membrane

Draw a hard line. Inside the line: dumb bytes. Outside the line: anything smart.

The blind substrate is built around one rule, enforced rigidly: nothing inside the relay's "blind membrane" is allowed to know anything about your app. Not your username, not your community name, not what kind of record it is, not who you replied to. Inside the membrane, the relay sees only random-looking bytes at random-looking addresses. (This is the substrate's target behavior — going live on the 2-relay cutover now. See the companion Honest Stack doc for the exact status of each claim.)

The payoff

If the relay can't tell peerit apart from a recipe app or a chat app, then adding a new app is becoming zero-config — no namespace to register, no schema to deploy, no operator approval. Anyone can build an app on the substrate without asking anyone's permission, because the relay genuinely doesn't know what's running on top of it.

Status: this is the substrate's defining property, and it's what the 2-relay cutover activates. Becomes fully true (Shipped) once the cutover converges and a second conforming app is stood up without relay-side config.

03What a "cell" actually is

Think of a cell as a single locked box, dropped off at a random locker.

When peerit (or any app) wants to store a record — say, your post — it doesn't send the post to the relay. It does something more careful:

  1. Takes your signed record (the post, already signed by your key)
  2. Wraps it in a layer of encryption with a brand-new random key, just for this one storage event
  3. Pads it to a fixed size, so every cell looks the same shape
  4. Generates a random storage address (called a "slot") that has nothing to do with the content
  5. Hands the relay an opaque locked box at a random locker number

Why fresh keys every time?

Each cell is encrypted with a brand-new random key, never reused. The same record, stored twice, produces completely different ciphertext. This is deliberate: it defeats convergent encryption attacks where an operator could guess a post's content and confirm the guess by comparing hashes. Random keys cost you deduplication — but deduplication is exactly the leak you don't want.

Why random slots?

The storage address ("slot") is derived from a coarse time bucket and a freshly generated "create" key. It has nothing to do with the content. Not the author, not the community, not the post ID. An operator looking at the filesystem sees a sea of random-looking addresses, with no way to group them by app or author without the client's read capability.

The capability you keep

To read a cell back, the client needs a read capability: the slot address plus the decryption key. This capability is never stored on the relay — it lives only in the client, threaded through encrypted "next pointers" between cells. Without the capability, the bytes are unreadable; with it, the client can follow the chain and reconstruct the app's logical state.

04Cells have runtimes

Same blind substrate, two different ways to ride on it — depending on what your device can actually do.

The substrate is the same; the shape of the cells depends on the runtime. There are two profiles, because a normal web browser and a native app have very different capabilities:

Both profiles speak the same protocol to the relay — the same five service families, the same dispatch frames, the same receipts. What differs is how the client packages its data before calling those services:

Blind Cells (browser)Blind Core (native)
Unit storedOne fixed-size ciphertext boxEncrypted blocks of a Hypercore
ConnectionOne-off HTTP requestsLive replication session
AddressRandom per-cell slotOpaque core/discovery key
Best forReading posts, posting a commentAlways-on sync, large feeds
Same inner data?YES — same signed records, same app logic

The unifying trick

Your app writes the same signed records either way. The substrate adapter decides whether to chop them into cells (browser) or stream them as encrypted core blocks (native). The app doesn't know or care which runtime a given reader is using — it just sees its own signed data flowing back, verified signature by signature.

05The honesty ladder (G0–G5)

"Blind" is not one property. It's a ladder, and you have to say which rung you're on.

The single most important rule in the whole spec: the word "blind" without a guarantee level is forbidden. Every claim has to name the highest level it actually proves. Here's the ladder, in plain terms:

G0
Integrity — "you can't fake me" A relay cannot make your client accept forged or altered bytes. Signatures catch it. This is the floor; everything above it is bonus.
G1
Payload opacity — "you can't read my post" The bytes the relay receives are randomized ciphertext, and it has no key to decrypt them. (Caveat: it can't stop someone intentionally uploading plaintext — but a conforming app never will.)
G2-S
Storage-schema opacity — "you can't see my graph" The relay's stored state contains no app name, no author key, no community, no record type, no graph edge. This is the rung that kills the social-graph leak from §01.
G2-W
Wire opacity — "you can't even tell it's my app" The storage relay also receives no app-identifying request metadata (Origin header, app path, etc.) during operation. Requires a split transport (see §06).
G3
At-rest unlinkability — "you can't link my cells" Cells use rotating pseudorandom slots and randomized encryption, so there's no deterministic key the relay can use to group them by author or app. Statistical correlation (timing, size, volume) may still leak.
G4-T
Route separation — "you can't trace the request to me" The storage role can't bind the operation to your network address, because an independently operated transport (OHTTP, Protomux split, Tor) sits between you and it.
G4-I
Read-interest privacy — "you can't tell which post I read" The relay can't distinguish which logical item you requested within an anonymity set. Requires bucket downloads, PIR, or ORAM. Hard.
G5
Active-reader resistance — "you can't read me even by joining as a reader" NOT ACHIEVABLE FOR PUBLIC CONTENT For a public forum, the read key reaches everyone — including an operator who joins as a reader. They can decrypt anything any public reader can. peerit explicitly fails this rung and says so.

The G5 honesty test — and why it matters

For public content, G5 is mathematically impossible. The read key has to reach every reader; an operator who wants to snoop can simply obtain it and run the client. Any product that claims "the operator can't read your public posts" is lying or confused. The spec requires the negative test: prove that an operator acting as a reader can identify slots, follow the capability chain, group authors, and decrypt the public content. Then say so plainly.

What the blind substrate does give you, honestly: no passive reading, no semantic enumeration, content-neutral storage, and deniability. Not secrecy. The win is that the operator has to affirmatively work to read anything, and can't build a passive index of the graph at rest.

06The five things relays can do

The strict daemon speaks exactly five "families" of operations. That's it.

Inside the membrane, the relay is deliberately dumb. It can't do search, can't rank posts, can't moderate, can't tell apps apart. It can do exactly five things — and they're all generic infrastructure, not app logic:

FamilyWhat it does (in human terms)App-relevant?
DESCRIBE"Hi, I'm relay X. Here's what I can do, signed by me." — capability advertisement.no app info
CELLStore / fetch / prove / delete a fixed locked box at a random address.no app info
INBOXA rotating, fixed-frame mailbox for opaque discovery announcements.no app info
CORESponsor / mirror an encrypted Hypercore for native runtimes.no app info
FORWARDA bounded opaque pipe to a next hop — used by split transports.no app info

That's the entire surface. Notably absent from the strict daemon:

  • No SEARCH — search is a client-side operation over your locally decrypted index
  • No MODERATE — moderation is signed client-honored overlays, not relay-side deletion
  • No DIRECTORY — no per-app namespace, no author directory, no heads endpoint
  • No REPAIR — repair is a client-side composition over CELL ops
  • No NOTIFY — no app-specific push; use generic inbox watches

The conformance gate

If supporting your app requires the relay to add a plugin, a schema, a namespace, a CORS domain edit, a metric label, a restart, or operator approval — your service is outside the membrane. It can coexist on the same machine, but only on a separate process with separate credentials and a separate claim boundary. The strict client path will never reach it.

How transports plug in

The five families are transport-neutral. The same canonical message can ride over:

  • direct-blind — client → storage over HTTPS or Noise. Lowest latency, but the storage sees your IP/Origin.
  • split-web (OHTTP) — browser → ingress → gateway/storage. The ingress sees you but not the request; the gateway sees the request but not you. G4-T under non-collusion
  • split-native (Protomux/MASQUE) — native client → entry → exit → storage. Same separation, native-grade.
  • tor-full — client → v3 onion → blind endpoint. Strictest source-location privacy, fails closed.

Transport changes who can correlate a request. It never changes storage authority, application truth, or what the ciphertext means.

07A post's journey, step by step

Alice posts "hello world" to r/peerit. Here's what actually happens.

Alice writes and signs the post

In her client (browser or PearBrowser), she types the post. Her app creates a signed record: post!peerit!a3f…, signed with her Ed25519 key. This record never leaves her device in plaintext.

The blind client wraps it

The @hiverelay/blind-client SDK takes the signed record and packages it: encrypts with a fresh AES-256-GCM key, pads to a fixed size class, generates random create/renew/drop keys, and derives a random slot address.

Cell is created on the relay

The client calls CELL.PUT with the opaque ciphertext. The relay stores it at the random slot, returns a signed receipt, and records generic accounting (size class, lease epoch, byte usage). The relay knows it stored something — not what, not for whom, not for which app.

This is the blind-cells protocol going live in the cutover. Until it converges, peerit runs on the legacy OutboxLog path (v2 opaque keys, partial blindness).

The read capability is threaded

The client encrypts the read capability (slot + decryption key) into a "next pointer" and stores that in another cell — or publishes it through an encrypted inbox. The capability chain lives entirely in ciphertext on the relay; only readers with the bootstrap capability can follow it.

Bob wants to read Alice's post

Bob's client has the public bootstrap capability for r/peerit. It follows the encrypted chain, fetches the relevant cells via CELL.GET, decrypts each with its read capability, and reconstructs the post locally.

Bob's client verifies the signature

Now Bob's client has Alice's signed record in plaintext. It checks: does the key match the claimed author? Does the signature verify? Does the storage key match what the record's own fields say it should be? Only if all three pass does the post enter Bob's local feed.

The relay is bypassed for trust

At no point did Bob's client trust the relay for authenticity. The relay moved bytes and gave receipts. Authority came from Alice's signature, verified locally. A malicious relay could have withheld the cell or served a stale copy — but it could never have forged Alice's post or edited her words.

08What your operator can and can't see

The honest table. No spin. Describes what the substrate achieves once the cutover converges — badge each row against current status before quoting it publicly.

The operator…CanCannot
Looking at stored bytes at rest See ciphertext blobs at random addresses, size classes, lease epochs, byte counts Read content, identify the app, identify the author, see the community, walk the graph by key prefix
Watching a direct browser request See your IP, the browser Origin header, request timing/volume, the random slot you asked for Decrypt the payload, tell which app you're using behind a split transport
Joining as a public reader Obtain the public read capability, follow the chain, decrypt all public content, group authors by their public keys Forge posts, edit records, impersonate authors, silently delete across the network
Running their own copy of the relay software See the protocol code, the storage engine, the accounting logic Find any app-specific code path, namespace, or "peerit mode" — there isn't one in the strict daemon
Colluding with another relay Compare slot timings, sizes, IPs, receipt patterns; statistically correlate activity Recover plaintext without the read capability, or break the encryption itself

Metadata still leaks

This is the part every "encrypted and anonymous" product hand-waves. The blind substrate reduces what leaks, but it does not eliminate leakage. Sizes, timing, access patterns, IP addresses (without a split transport), and total volume are all still observable. Stronger privacy (G4-I: "you can't tell which post I read") needs bucket downloads or PIR, which the substrate supports but does not ship by default.

If a product claims anonymity from "encrypted bytes + Tor support" alone, that's marketing. The spec forbids unqualified "blind" claims for exactly this reason.

09What this does not do

The hard ceilings. Named plainly, because pretending otherwise is the original sin of this space.

  1. It does not hide public content from an operator who joins as a reader. That's G5, and G5 is mathematically impossible for public data. The read key has to reach every reader; an operator can always obtain it. The win is deniability and no passive index — not secrecy.
  2. It does not prove hardware actually erased data. If an operator keeps a copy despite issuing a DROP, the substrate can't prove the bytes are gone from disk. Cryptographic erasure is a claim, not a physics guarantee.
  3. It does not solve Sybil. Identities are cheap to mint. The substrate doesn't know about "users" at all — it just moves bytes. Vote-weighting, one-human-one-vote, and reputation live at the app layer, not here.
  4. It does not provide a global name registry. "r/peerit" is an app concept. The substrate has no opinion on names; naming is app-level and subject to Zooko's triangle (human-readable, decentralized, squat-proof — pick two).
  5. It does not resist a global passive observer. An adversary watching the whole network can correlate timing and volume. Defeating that needs mixnets or cover traffic, which are research-grade and explicitly out of scope for v1.
  6. It does not do semantic search, ranking, or moderation. Those are app functions. The relay cannot rank posts because it cannot read them. All search is local-first, over your own decrypted index.
  7. It does not make the website unblockable. Multi-relay resists relay seizure and rollback, but a censor blocking the single origin serving the web bundle still wins. The fix (inlining the signed roster into the page) is still ahead.
  8. It does not give you password recovery. No accounts means no "forgot password" button. Your key is your identity; lose both the key and your backup phrase, and it's gone. PearBrowser gives you a 12-word BIP-39 phrase, but that's the floor.

Why this honesty matters

Every ceiling on this list is a place where a less honest product would claim a feature it doesn't have. The blind substrate's whole value proposition is that the claims it does make are load-bearing and verifiable — not that it solves every problem. If you read one thing in this document, read this list. The ceilings are the contract.

10Glossary

Blind substrate
The whole system described here. A storage layer that holds app data without being able to interpret it. "Blind" = the relay can't see app meaning; "substrate" = it's a foundation many apps can build on, not a peerit-specific backend.
Strict membrane
The hard line between "outside" (app-owned, knows everything) and "inside" (relay-operated, knows nothing). App info cannot cross it. Adding a new app requires no relay changes precisely because nothing app-specific ever enters the membrane. (Going live with the 2-relay cutover — this is the substrate's defining property, now activating.)
Cell
One fixed-size encrypted storage unit. The relay sees opaque ciphertext at a random address. The client sees a locked box it can open with a read capability.
Slot
The random storage address of a cell. Derived from a coarse time bucket and a one-time "create" key. Has nothing to do with the content — not the author, not the app, not the record type.
Read capability
The slot address plus the decryption key. Held only by the client, threaded through encrypted next-pointers. Never stored on the relay. Without it, the bytes are unreadable.
Write capability
One-time create key plus independent renew/drop keys for a cell. Lets the writer manage the cell's lifecycle without giving them read access to the content.
Capsule
The encrypted inner record plus encrypted routing/chain data and padding, before it becomes a cell. The unit of app-level encryption.
Inner record
The application-defined signed bytes. For peerit: a post, vote, comment, identity, or mod action, signed by its author's Ed25519 key. Lives only inside ciphertext.
Blind Cells vs Blind Core
The two runtime profiles. Blind Cells = fixed boxes for browsers; Blind Core = encrypted Hypercore streaming for native runtimes. Same protocol, same five verbs, different shape.
Conforming producer
A client that encrypts and pads correctly before calling the substrate. The substrate can't stop a malicious client from uploading plaintext, but no conforming app ever will. (Think: a public byte store can't detect intentional plaintext.)
Guarantee ladder (G0–G5)
The levels of blindness a claim can prove. Every "blind" claim must name its highest rung. G0 = can't fake. G1 = can't read payload. G2 = can't see schema. G3 = can't link cells. G4 = can't trace or identify reads. G5 = can't read even as a reader (impossible for public).
The five families
DESCRIBE, CELL, INBOX, CORE, FORWARD. The complete protocol surface a strict relay exposes. No search, no moderate, no directory — those are client-side.
Split transport
A multi-hop path (OHTTP, Protomux split, Tor) that separates "who is asking" from "what is being asked for." Required for G4-T (route separation). The substrate's privacy ceiling rises with the transport you choose.
Bootstrap capability
The encrypted entry point that lets a reader start following the capability chain. For public peerit it's distributed outside the storage protocol; for private apps only to members.
Operator diversity
Evidence that different roles (entry, exit, storage) are run by different parties. Different keys, hosts, ASNs, regions are signals — never cryptographic proof of independence.

Terminology. This document uses "relay" throughout, never "server." The relay is a dumb pipe that carries blind bytes — it owns nothing, interprets nothing, and is trusted for nothing. "Serverless" is reserved for PearBrowser only, where no relay is in the path.
