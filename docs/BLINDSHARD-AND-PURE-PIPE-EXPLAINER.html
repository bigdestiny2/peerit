Read the first explainer first

This document assumes you've read The Blind Substrate, explained for humans. The substrate (Layer 1) makes the storage keys blind — so the relay can't enumerate the social graph. BlindShard (Layer 2, this doc) makes the post bodies blind — so no single operator holds a readable, complete, or linkable copy of what you wrote. The pure pipe (the end state) takes it further: the founder's own box stops serving content entirely.

01Why bodies need their own layer

The substrate blinded the graph. The bodies — the actual words people wrote — were still readable on a single box.

After the blind substrate ships, a relay can no longer tell who posted where by reading storage keys. But the post bodies themselves — the markdown, the rants, the links — are a separate problem. Even with opaque keys, three things can still go wrong:

  1. The relay that holds an encrypted cell also holds the key to decrypt it (shipped in the manifest).
  2. One seizure of one box gets a complete, readable copy of every body that box stores.
  3. One subpoena of one operator gets the same.

BlindShard exists to break that co-location. The goal is brutally specific: arrange things so that no single operator, holding only their own disk, can read, complete, or even attribute a post body. Plausible deniability becomes a real legal posture, not a slogan.

02BlindShard in one picture

Encrypt the body once. Split the key across many operators. Spread the ciphertext so no one box holds enough to matter.

The construction that shipped (and it's different from the original whitepaper — see §03) does three things to each post body over a size threshold:

Three properties fall out of this shape, and they're the whole point:

  • No single operator has a readable body. The ciphertext relay has no key; the key-share relays have no ciphertext.
  • No single operator has a complete body. Reassembling requires gathering K key-shares from different operators, then fetching the ciphertext, then decrypting. That's an affirmative act against the whole network — not something any one disk does at rest.
  • No single operator can attribute a body. The ciphertext is content-addressed by a hash of itself (shard:<blake2b>), with no link to author, community, or post. The share fragments are equally opaque.

03Split the key, not the data

A small but important detail — and a place where the shipped code differs from the original design doc.

The original BlindShard design (now superseded) used Reed-Solomon erasure coding on the ciphertext itself — split the locked box into N fragments, any K of which could reconstruct the box. That's the Tahoe-LAFS / Storj pattern, and it works.

What actually shipped is different and better for this use case: PVSS-split the AES key, and store the whole ciphertext as a single shard. The key, not the data, gets distributed across the cohort.

Original design (superseded)What shipped
What gets split The ciphertext (Reed-Solomon k-of-N fragments) The AES key (PVSS k-of-N shares)
Ciphertext location Distributed as N fragments, <K per relay One whole copy, stored as shard:0
Key location Published in the manifest (public) Never published — split across cohort, hidden below threshold
Below K shares Cannot reconstruct the ciphertext Cannot reconstruct the key stronger

Crypto dep WASM Reed-Solomon sodium-universal + vendored @noble/* PVSS-secp256k1

Why the key-split is stronger

In the erasure-code scheme, gathering K ciphertext fragments reassembles the locked box — and the key was already public in the manifest, so any reader could decrypt. In the PVSS scheme, gathering fewer than K key-shares yields computationally nothing about the key. The ciphertext is sitting right there, but it's mathematically opaque until you cross the threshold. For a public forum where the read key must reach every reader, this is a meaningful upgrade in passive-at-rest blindness.

The honest framing: this is still not secrecy for public content. A determined party (including the operator) who obtains the public read capability can still gather K shares and reconstruct. But the bar moves from "I can grep my own disk" to "I must coordinate an attack against K independent operators." That bar is the difference between liability and deniability.

04A body's journey, step by step

Alice writes a long post. Here's what happens to the body specifically.

Alice writes and signs the post metadata

Her client creates the post record — community, title, timestamp, author signature — and signs it with her Ed25519 key. This metadata is small and stays inline in the outbox record (sealed under the v2 read-key, as in the substrate explainer).

Size gate decides: inline or shard?

Short bodies (votes, short comments, small posts) stay inline as sealed values — no sharding overhead. Only bodies above a threshold (roughly multi-KB) enter the BlindShard pipeline. This keeps the hot path cheap.

Generate a fresh AES key and encrypt the body

The client picks a brand-new random 256-bit AES key K and encrypts the body to ciphertext C using AES-256-GCM. K is never reused; the same body written twice produces different ciphertext.

PVSS-split the key into N shares

Using Publicly Verifiable Secret Sharing, K is split into N shares such that any K of them can reconstruct K, but fewer than K reveals computationally nothing. Each share is small (a few hundred bytes).

Store the ciphertext as a shard

The whole ciphertext C is written as shard:0 — addressed by blake2b-256(C), a content hash that has no link to author or post. The relay that holds it sees opaque bytes at a random-looking address.

Distribute the key shares across the cohort

Each of the N key shares is written to a different cohort relay, by index, under a signed custody intent. No single cohort relay holds more than one share; the ciphertext relay holds zero shares.

Write a keyless manifest into the signed outbox record

The post's outbox record carries a small manifest: { ciphertextShard, k, n, shareIndices } — but not the key. The manifest is signed by Alice and sealed under the read-key like the rest of the record. It tells a reader where to look, not how to decrypt.

05How a reader reassembles

Bob opens Alice's post. His client reverses the journey — locally, in his browser.

Fetch the signed outbox record

Bob's client gets Alice's post record from the outbox relay — same as any post. It verifies Alice's signature and unseals the record under the read-key.

Read the keyless manifest

The manifest tells Bob's client where the ciphertext shard lives and where the K key-shares are distributed across the cohort. No key yet — just pointers.

Gather K key-shares from the cohort

Bob's client fetches any K of the N shares from the cohort relays. Because PVSS is threshold-based, any K of the N will do — if relay D is down, fetch from E instead.

Reconstruct the AES key locally

Bob's client combines the K shares to reconstruct the original AES key K. This happens entirely in Bob's browser — no relay ever sees the reconstructed key.

Fetch the ciphertext shard

Bob's client fetches shard:0 — the locked box — from the ciphertext relay. It's just opaque bytes; harmless on its own.

Decrypt and verify

With the key and the ciphertext, Bob's client decrypts the body and verifies it against the manifest's hash. If anything was tampered with — wrong share, substituted ciphertext, forged manifest — the check fails and Bob's client routes around the bad relay.

The key insight: at no point did any relay hold both the key and the ciphertext. Only Bob's browser, after gathering from multiple independent operators, reconstructs the key. The reconstruction is a reader-side act, not a server-side state.

The lazy hydration trick

None of this happens for posts you scroll past. The feed renders titles, authors, and vote counts from the small sealed records — no shard fetches needed. Body reassembly happens only when you open a post. So the dispersal layer adds zero cost to feed browsing; it only kicks in for the post you're actually reading.

06The pure pipe end state

The north star. The founder's own box stops serving content entirely.

BlindShard blinds the bodies. The substrate blinds the graph. But there's still a relay somewhere aggregating outboxes and answering feed queries — an /api server that, even if it can't read what it serves, is still a content-serving index. The pure pipe is the move that dissolves even that role.

Under the pure pipe:

  • The founder's box is one optional pure pipe among many — or it can be turned off entirely, leaning on the fleet's dht-relay-ws endpoints.
  • The reader's browser runs a real HyperDHT node and does its own discovery, its own shard gathering, its own reassembly.
  • Durable storage lives on the opt-in blind-seeder fleet + user devices, holding blind erasure shards.
  • No operator — including the founder — aggregates or serves readable or complete content. The only thing anyone holds at rest is blind, incomplete, content-hashed fragments.

The liability ladder, completed

Each rung strictly shrinks what the founder's box holds and can be blamed for:

v1 baseline
"I host every readable post on one box." Plaintext JSON, full social graph. Maximum liability.
substrate
"I host opaque-keyed cells I can't enumerate by graph." Graph blinded; bodies still readable if the relay has the key.
BlindShard
"I host meaningless fragments I can't read, link, or reassemble alone." Bodies dispersed; no single relay has a complete readable item.
pure pipe
"I transport ciphertext frames between online peers. I store and serve nothing." The founder's box is a dumb pipe; the network's blind seeders hold the fragments.

07Reader-side aggregation

The job of "build the feed" moves out of any server and into each reader's device.

This is the part that sounds impossible until you see it. In the pure-pipe end state, there is no server that holds a list of posts or answers queries like "give me the front page." Instead, each reader's browser does the work itself, in real time:

The pipe's only job is to carry encrypted frames between peers who chose to be online. It doesn't know what those frames contain. It doesn't index them. It doesn't answer queries. It's a telephone wire, not a switchboard operator.

Why this is a real shift, not a relabeling

Moving aggregation into the reader means there is no longer a server to subpoena for "the feed." There is no operator who can be compelled to produce "all posts in r/peerit" because no operator holds that view. Each reader holds the view they personally assembled, after gathering from many independent sources. The network's intelligence is distributed across readers, not concentrated in any relay.

08Why this resists takedown

To kill a post, you have to kill K independent operators simultaneously. To kill the site, you have to kill the readers.

Takedown resistance comes from the combination of dispersal and reader-side aggregation. Here's what an attacker actually faces at each layer:

Killing a single post

Attacker actionResult
Seize the ciphertext relayThe locked box is gone — but readers route around it. Any other seeder holding a copy serves it. And without the key, the box was unreadable anyway.
Seize one cohort relayOne key-share is gone. Readers fetch a different share from another cohort member. K-of-N tolerates up to N−K failures.
Block the manifest outboxReaders can't find pointers. Mitigated by inline-signed-roster distribution across many mirrors (still a work in progress).
Subpoena one operatorThey hand over opaque bytes they can't read or attribute. Plausible deniability is a real legal posture.
Compel K operators to colludeThis works. Gathering K shares + the ciphertext + the manifest reconstructs the body. The defense is operator independence — K operators in K jurisdictions.

Killing the whole site

The pure-pipe architecture makes this remarkably hard:

  • There's no "the site" to seize. The founder's VPS is one optional pipe among many. Turn it off and readers reach the fleet's other dht-relay-ws endpoints.
  • There's no central index to subpoena. The feed is assembled in each reader's browser. There's no server that holds "the front page."
  • There's no DNS to revoke. Sites are addressed by Hyperdrive keys, not domains. The peerit.site URL is a convenience on-ramp, not the network's address.
  • There's no single operator to pressure. The fleet is permissionless — anyone can run a blind seeder, and adding seeders strengthens the network without asking anyone's permission.

The honest ceiling on takedown resistance

This is not impunity. Three real attacks still work:

  1. Block the entry point. A censor that blocks the single origin serving the web bundle still wins — until the signed roster is inlined into the page and the bundle is mirrored across IPFS / extensions / other origins. This work is ongoing.
  2. Collude across K operators. If K cohort members are compelled (or paid) to cooperate, reconstruction is possible. Defense is jurisdictional diversity, not cryptography.
  3. Go after the readers. A censor that blocks all dht-relay-ws endpoints in their network blocks all readers. The pure pipe doesn't help here — the readers are the network.

What the architecture does promise: no single legal action, against any single operator, can take down a post or the site. That's a real and meaningful bar — but it's not "unstoppable." Don't market it as such.

09Who's hosting it? (nobody knows)

The plausible-deniability story, told honestly.

Here's the question every relay operator should be able to answer with a straight face: "what am I hosting?" Under BlindShard + the pure pipe, the honest answer is striking:

"I run an opt-in availability relay. Under shard:* I hold content-hash-addressed opaque bytes I cannot decrypt (no keys), cannot attribute to any post or user (the address is a hash of the bytes), and cannot reassemble alone (I never hold enough fragments of any one item). Reconstructing anything requires deliberately fetching the public manifest AND collecting K fragments from OTHER operators — an affirmative act against the whole network, not something my disk does at rest."

That's the Freenet-CHK grade posture. Liability shrinks from "I host this user's readable post" to "I host meaningless, incomplete, encrypted fragments." Selective censorship-by-meaning becomes impossible because the relay has no content index.

Why "nobody knows who's hosting what"

Three properties combine to make attribution genuinely hard:

  • Content-addressed storage. A shard is stored at blake2b-256(ciphertext) — a hash of its own bytes. That address has no link to author, community, or post. An operator looking at their disk sees a sea of random hashes.
  • Author-decoupled surface. The shard store is not gated by data._k === appId the way the old outbox was. Shards are anonymous blobs, not part of any author's enumerable outbox. There's no "show me everything alice stored" query.
  • No app awareness. The strict substrate (Layer 1) has no concept of "peerit" or any other app. A relay operator can't tell whether they're hosting fragments of a forum, a chat app, or a recipe collection — the bytes look identical.

What deniability is — and is not

It is: a real legal posture. "I hold opaque bytes I cannot read or attribute" is a defensible position in a way that "I host alice's readable post about X" is not. The operator must affirmatively act (gather K shares from other operators, fetch the manifest, run the reader) to learn anything — and that act leaves evidence.

It is not: cryptographic secrecy. A determined party who obtains the public read capability can reconstruct. A court that compels K operators can reconstruct. The win is that ordinary operation exposes nothing — not that no attack ever could.

Forbidden marketing: "the operator can't read your posts." That's a lie. The honest claim is "the operator must affirmatively reconstruct, in coordination with other operators, to read anything — and ordinary disk-grep yields nothing."

10Honest ceilings

What BlindShard and the pure pipe do not achieve. Read these before believing any claim.

  1. Not secrecy for public content. The read key reaches every reader, including an operator acting as a reader. A determined party can always reconstruct by gathering K shares. The win is deniability and non-readability-in-isolation, not confidentiality.
  2. Not metadata blindness. Shard sizes, request timing, IP addresses (without a split transport), total volume, and per-author record counts all still leak. Sharding hides bodies, not associations.
  3. Not immunity to collusion. If K cohort operators cooperate (willingly or under compulsion), they can reconstruct any body. Defense is jurisdictional and operator diversity — not cryptography.
  4. Not impunity from blocking. A censor that blocks the single origin serving the web bundle still wins, until the signed roster is inlined and the bundle is mirrored. Sharding resists seizure, not blocking.
  5. Not durability until custody receipts ship. Today, shard placement is fire-and-forget — there's no quorum receipt confirming that K independent operators actually persisted their shares. Until that lands, the user's device cache is the real backstop.
  6. Not deployed at scale yet. The shipped code is wired but dispersal is disabled in production (empty shard roster). Real dispersal requires ≥3 (ideally ≥6) independent operators — a deployment property, not a code property. Until then, peerit runs with single-relay v2 opaque logs, which blind the graph but not the bodies.
  7. Not a substitute for the signed-release chain. Even with perfect dispersal, a compromised origin can serve malicious JavaScript that exfiltrates keys. The signed-release chain (verifying the bundle against an offline key) is the complementary defense on the code side. Both layers are needed.
  8. Not anonymous. Operators see IPs (without Tor), request patterns, and connection metadata. Privacy against a global passive observer is explicitly out of scope and requires mixnets or cover traffic that v1 does not provide.

If you read one thing, read this

The blind substrate + BlindShard + pure pipe stack is a liability-reduction and takedown-resistance architecture, not a secrecy or anonymity architecture. It shrinks what any single operator holds, raises the bar for reconstruction from "grep my own disk" to "coordinate an attack across K independent operators," and moves the feed-building job out of any seizable server. It does not make public posts secret. It does not hide metadata. It does not defeat a determined global adversary.

Claim past these ceilings and you've become the kind of project this architecture was built to be different from.

11Glossary

BlindShard
The body-dispersal layer. Encrypts post bodies, splits the AES key across a cohort of relays, and stores the ciphertext separately so no single operator holds a readable, complete, or linkable copy.
PVSS (Publicly Verifiable Secret Sharing)
The cryptographic technique used to split the AES key. Any K of N shares can reconstruct the key; fewer than K reveals computationally nothing. "Publicly verifiable" means anyone can check that a share is well-formed without learning the secret.
Cohort
The group of independent relay operators who each hold one key-share. The cohort is the unit of reconstruction — you need K of them to recover a key.
Threshold (K of N)
The reconstruction parameters. N shares are distributed; any K reconstruct the key. Typical: K=3, N=5 — tolerates 2 missing operators.
Pure pipe
The end state where the founder's VPS is a dumb byte-pipe (dht-relay-ws) that carries encrypted frames but stores and serves nothing. Readers aggregate for themselves.
Reader-side aggregation
When each reader's browser runs a real HyperDHT node and builds the feed itself — discovering authors, pulling shards, reassembling bodies — instead of querying a central server.
Blind seeder fleet
The permissionless network of opt-in operators who hold blind erasure shards. The fleet is what makes the pure pipe durable — without enough independent seeders, there's nothing to read.
Content-addressed storage
Storing bytes at an address derived from a hash of the bytes themselves (here, blake2b-256). The address has no link to author or content meaning — it's just a fingerprint of the data.
Author-decoupled
A storage surface that does not gate writes by data._k === appId. Shards are anonymous blobs, not part of any author's enumerable outbox. There's no "show me everything alice stored" query.
Custody intent / receipt
A signed statement from a relay that it holds a specific share. Turns fire-and-forget placement into acknowledged, auditable storage. Phase 4 work.
Lazy hydration
The optimization where body reassembly happens only when a post is opened, not when the feed renders. Keeps the dispersal layer's cost off the hot path.
Plausible deniability
The legal posture: "I hold opaque bytes I cannot read or attribute." Defensible because the operator must affirmatively act (coordinate with other operators) to learn anything.
Liability ladder
The four-rung progression: v1 (readable host) → substrate (blind graph) → BlindShard (blind bodies) → pure pipe (serve nothing). Each rung strictly shrinks what the founder's box holds and can be blamed for.
Manifest
The small signed record that tells a reader where to find the ciphertext shard and the K key-shares. Crucially, the shipped manifest carries no key — only pointers.
