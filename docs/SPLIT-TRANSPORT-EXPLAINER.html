Split Transport,
explained simply

Right now, when you read a post on peerit, the relay knows two things: who you are (your IP address) and what you asked for (which post). Split transport is how we separate those two things so no single relay knows both.

The one-sentence version

Split transport puts a middleman between you and the storage relay. The middleman knows who you are but not what you asked for. The storage relay knows what you asked for but not who you are. Neither has the full picture.

01The problem: the relay sees everything

Imagine you're at a library. You walk up to the desk and say "I want to borrow the book about gardening." The librarian now knows two things:

  • Who you are — they can see your face
  • What you're interested in — gardening

That's how peerit works today. When your browser asks the relay for a post, the relay sees:

  • Your IP address (who you are on the internet)
  • The post you requested (what you're reading)
  • The time you asked

The relay can't read the content of the post (that's encrypted — the blind substrate handles that). But it CAN build a profile: "this IP address reads a lot of posts in r/privacy, asks for posts by Alice at 3am, and votes on crypto content." That's metadata. It leaks.

02The fix: split "who" from "what"

The mailbox analogy

Instead of walking up to the librarian yourself, you send a friend to the desk. The friend knows who you are (they're your friend) but the note they hand the librarian is in a sealed envelope — the friend can't read it. The librarian opens the envelope, gets the request ("gardening book"), but has no idea who really asked — they only see your friend, who's running errands for dozens of people.

Your friend is the ingress. The librarian is the storage relay. The sealed envelope is OHTTP encryption. Neither person has the full picture.

03How the "sealed envelope" works

The sealed envelope isn't a metaphor — it's a real cryptographic protocol called OHTTP (Oblivious HTTP, RFC 9458). It's an internet standard, not something we invented. Here's what happens when you click on a post:

  1. Your browser encrypts the request using a public key published by the storage relay. This encryption is called HPKE (Hybrid Public Key Encryption, RFC 9180 — another standard). Once encrypted, only the storage relay can open it. The ingress can't read it.
  2. Your browser sends the encrypted blob to the ingress. The ingress sees your IP address and sees that you sent something, but it can't tell what's inside.
  3. The ingress strips your identity. It removes your IP, your browser's Origin header, cookies, referer — everything that could identify you or the app you're using. Then it forwards just the encrypted blob to the storage relay.
  4. The storage relay decrypts the request. Now it knows "someone wants post a3f…" — but the only IP it sees is the ingress's IP, not yours. It has no idea who actually asked.
  5. The response goes back the same way — encrypted, through the ingress, back to your browser. The ingress can't read the response either.

The key insight

The ingress and the storage relay must be run by different operators. If the same person runs both, they can compare notes and link your IP to your request. That's the one assumption the whole thing rests on — and it's why we need multiple independent relay operators, not just one.

04Two flavors: browser and native

The split works differently depending on what kind of app you're using:

For browsers (Chrome, Firefox, Safari) — OHTTP

Browsers can't do raw network tricks, so they use the OHTTP path described above. Your browser encrypts the request, sends it to an ingress (a web endpoint), which strips your identity and forwards it to the storage relay. Everything happens over normal HTTPS — no special browser plugins needed.

The one tricky part: browsers automatically send an Origin header that says "this request came from peerit.site." To hide that, the OHTTP client runs inside a special sandboxed iframe that sends Origin: null instead. This works in theory; we need to verify it on the wire across all three browser engines before claiming it's airtight.

For native apps (PearBrowser, Bare, Node) — Protomux split

Native apps have real network access — UDP sockets, real connections. They use a different path: a two-hop Noise tunnel. Your app connects to an entry relay, which forwards through an exit relay to the storage. The entry knows your IP but not what you're asking for (it's encrypted). The exit knows what's being asked but not your IP (it only sees the entry relay). The payload is end-to-end encrypted with Noise — neither relay can read it.

This reuses HiveRelay's existing forward-relay.js — the byte-bridge that's already shipped. We're upgrading its framing to add flow control and circuit identities, not building something new.

05What this enables

Split transport moves peerit up the honesty ladder. Specifically it achieves two new rungs:

RungWhat it meansBefore split transportAfter
G2-W
Wire opacity		 The storage relay can't even tell which app you're using from the network traffic No — browser sends Origin: peerit.site Yes — Origin stripped; opaque request
G4-T
Route separation		 The storage relay can't bind your request to your IP address No — direct HTTPS exposes your IP Yes — ingress hides your IP from storage

In plain terms, after split transport ships, the storage relay knows less about you than your ISP does when you browse any normal website. It knows a post was requested, but not by whom.

What changes for the user

Nothing visible. The user opens peerit, reads posts, writes comments — exactly as before. The split transport runs underneath. The only visible change is a privacy indicator in the UI showing which tier they're on: "direct" (IP visible), "split" (IP hidden), or "onion" (Tor, strongest). The user doesn't need to understand any of this to benefit from it.

06What it does NOT do (the honest ceilings)

Split transport is a real improvement, but it's not magic. Here's what it doesn't achieve:

ClaimTrue?Why
"Your IP is hidden from the storage relay" Yes Under non-collusion (ingress ≠ storage operator)
"The ingress can't read your requests" Yes HPKE encryption — cryptographic, not just a policy
"The relay can't tell which post you're reading" No The storage relay decrypts the request and sees the slot. Hiding which slot you asked for needs PIR (Private Information Retrieval) — a harder, separate problem.
"Your traffic is anonymous" No Timing and volume are still observable. A determined adversary watching both the ingress and the storage could correlate traffic patterns. No cover traffic.
"It works against a global observer" No An adversary watching the entire network can see you → ingress → storage and connect the dots. This needs mixnets (like Nym), which are a different layer.
"No relay is needed" No The ingress IS a relay. It's just a blind one. Browsers always need a relay in the path — that's a hard platform limit.

The one assumption that matters

Everything rests on non-collusion: the ingress and the storage relay must be run by different operators who don't share data. If they're the same person, or if they secretly cooperate, the split is useless — they can link your IP to your request trivially.

This is why recruiting independent relay operators is the real prerequisite for split transport. The code is secondary; the operator diversity is what makes the privacy claim real.

07How it fits with everything else

Split transport is one layer in a stack of privacy protections. Here's how they compose:

Each layer protects against a different thing:

  • Signatures protect against forgery.
  • Blind substrate protects against reading your content at rest.
  • BlindShard protects against any single operator holding a complete readable body.
  • Split transport protects against linking your identity to your reading habits.
  • Nym/Tor would protect against timing correlation (future, much harder).

None of these layers makes the others redundant. You need all of them for the full picture. Split transport fills a specific gap: it's the layer that says "the relay knows what I asked for, but not that it was me asking."

08The honest one-liner

Split transport separates "who you are" from "what you asked for" so no single relay knows both. It uses standard encryption (OHTTP) to blind the middleman, and requires independent operators so the two halves can't be put back together. It hides your IP from the storage relay and your request content from the ingress — but it doesn't hide which post you're reading from the storage relay, and it doesn't resist timing analysis. It's one important layer in a stack of privacy protections, not the whole answer.

What you can say when it ships

  • "Your IP address is hidden from the relay that stores your data."
  • "The middleman can't read your requests — they're encrypted."
  • "Neither half of the path knows both who you are and what you're reading."

What you must NOT say

  • "It's anonymous." (Timing still leaks.)
  • "The relay can't see what you're reading." (It can — just not WHO is reading.)
  • "No relay is needed." (The ingress IS a relay — just a blind one.)
  • "It's untraceable." (A global observer can correlate traffic.)
